Keeping Your Kerman Water Heater Running Longer

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which reduces the risk of scalding and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on a descaling flush once a year in hard-water areas.

How old is your water heater? The serial number on the label usually encodes the manufacture date, and that date tells you a lot. Most tank models reach 8 to 12 years, heat pump models 10 to 15, and regularly descaled tankless units about 20. If your heater is past that range and also shows rusty water, rumbling, or dampness under the tank, it's a strong candidate for a planned replacement, and that's almost always cheaper and less stressful than an emergency replacement after a leak.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to excess pressure or thermal expansion with nowhere to go, both modest fixes. The same drip left alone for a season can keep stressing the tank until a seam gives way, and a small repair becomes a full replacement and a cleanup. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and ruined floors.

Typical Water Heater Issues in Kerman

Water heaters in and around Kerman deal with a familiar set of stresses. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be a real factor: dissolved minerals drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the popping and rumbling you may hear when the heater fires. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling is worth keeping up with where the water is hard.

Equipment age plays a big role too. When the sacrificial anode rod wears out, often well before the tank itself looks old, the tank begins corroding from within, and a drip from the bottom seam is usually the first visible sign. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: pilot assemblies, gas valves, and flues on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Code is another piece. Replacing an older heater often means updating the installation along the way: an expansion tank if the water system is closed, a relief valve drain line piped to an approved spot, a flue that drafts correctly on gas units, plus straps and a pan where required. Homes on well water have their own issues, such as extra sediment, iron staining, and the sulfur odor that sometimes develops when the water reacts with a standard anode rod. All of these is fixable, and most are much cheaper to address early. If something seems off, a diagnostic visit from our team can keep a small problem from turning into a flooded floor.
